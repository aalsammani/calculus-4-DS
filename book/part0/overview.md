# Chapter 1 · Mathematical Foundations

Every quantitative discipline is built on a small set of functions and the fluency to manipulate them. This opening chapter rebuilds that fluency deliberately — not as a review to be skimmed, but as the load-bearing floor for everything after it. Readers arrive at this material from many backgrounds: some saw this material a decade ago, some saw it in a different language or notation, some saw it well but have not *used* it since. The two sections here reset everyone to a common, precise baseline.

**What this chapter covers, and why it leads.**

- **Section 1.1 — Functions and Their Graphs.** The function concept itself: domain, range, notation, graphs, piecewise definitions, symmetry, transformations, composition, and inverses. Two skills installed here are used on nearly every later page: simplifying *difference quotients* (the algebra that becomes the derivative in Section 2.1) and *decomposing* functions into layers (the skill the chain rule of Section 2.3 automates).

- **Section 1.2 — Exponentials, Logarithms, and Trigonometry.** The three function families data science leans on hardest: exponentials for growth and decay, logarithms for undoing them (and for making multiplicative data readable), and sines and cosines for anything periodic. The number $e$ is developed honestly through compound interest, and the sinusoid's amplitude–period–phase anatomy is set up for its Fourier-series payoff in Section 4.3.

**How to study this chapter.** Work every Quick Check before moving on — they are calibrated so that a wrong answer points at a specific gap. Do the hand exercises *before* touching Python; the code sections exist to verify and extend hand work, never to replace it. If the algebra itself feels shaky (factoring, fractions, exponent rules), detour first through Appendix A's refresher, which exists for exactly this moment.

**Where it leads.** Section 2.1 turns the difference quotient into the derivative. Nothing in that leap is hard *if* the material here is genuinely fluent — and nearly everything in it is hard if it is not.
